Decoding: the same model, different writers

A language model gives a probability for every possible next token. How we choose from that list — greedily, or by sampling after reshaping it — decides whether the text is repetitive, sensible, varied or bizarre.

“After starting latanoprost, the patient’s pressure was …”

The twelve candidate logits are illustrative, chosen to look like a plausible model output; a real model scores its whole vocabulary of tens of thousands of tokens.

    What to notice

    Low temperature sharpens the distribution until sampling becomes greedy decoding: the same word every time. High temperature flattens it until nonsense candidates such as “purple” get real probability. Top-k and top-p leave the order unchanged and simply cut off the tail.

    Top-p adapts where top-k cannot: when the model is confident, a 0.9 nucleus may hold one or two tokens; when it is unsure, many. For factual clinical text, low temperature is usually right; for brainstorming, higher. None of these settings makes a wrong model right — they only choose among what it already believes (Chapter 145).
