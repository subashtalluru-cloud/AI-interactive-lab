Byte-pair encoding, one merge at a time

A tokenizer is learned, not designed. Start from single characters; repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair and glue it into a new token. Frequent words and suffixes emerge on their own.

What to notice

With the book’s corpus the first five merges are e+s, es+t, est+</w>, l+o, lo+w: the procedure discovers the suffix “-est” and the word “low” without any knowledge of English. A new word such as “lowest” then becomes two meaningful pieces.

Ties are broken by whichever pair appears first. Real tokenizers run tens of thousands of merges on bytes rather than characters, so any script or file can be represented — but words rare in the training text (many drug names, many non-English words) still split into more pieces, which costs more tokens and context.
