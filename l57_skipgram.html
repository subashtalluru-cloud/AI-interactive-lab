Word vectors from co-occurrence: skip-gram with negative sampling

Every word starts as a random point. For each word pair that appears together in the corpus, their vectors are pulled together; for random pairs, pushed apart. Nothing else is told to the model — yet words used in similar contexts end up near each other.

What to notice

The worked pair from Math Box 57.1: a real-pair score of 2.0 costs −ln σ(2.0) ≈ 0.127; a negative-pair score of 0.5 costs −ln σ(−0.5) ≈ 0.974. Training raises the first and lowers the second — tracked in the readout for “pressure”/“intraocular” and “pressure”/“bicycle”.

Set k = 0 and retrain: with only the “pull together” half, the easiest way to lower the loss is to make every score large, so vectors grow and drift into one direction. Topics separate less (in one run the between-topic cosine rose from 0.46 to 0.58, and the within-topic figures fell), and the model learns nothing about what does not belong together — the bicycle score rises instead of falling. In two dimensions the effect is modest; real embeddings use hundreds of dimensions and billions of words, but the mechanism is this one (Chapters 56–57). The same “pull real pairs, push random pairs” idea reappears as CLIP’s contrastive loss (Chapter 119).
