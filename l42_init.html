Why initial weights must be scaled

Send random signals through ten layers of freshly initialized weights. If each layer multiplies the signal’s size by more than 1, it explodes; by less than 1, it vanishes. The right initial spread keeps it steady.

What to notice

n = 256: SD 1 multiplies the signal by 16 per layer (10¹² after ten layers); SD 0.01 shrinks it to 0.16 per layer; SD 1/16 = 0.0625 keeps it at 1 (Math Box 42.1).

With ReLU, Xavier’s 1/√n lets the signal decay, because ReLU zeroes about half of each layer — He’s √(2/n) compensates. With tanh, large weights saturate the units: the signal cannot explode past 1, but the slopes collapse and gradients vanish instead (Chapters 36, 42, 54).
