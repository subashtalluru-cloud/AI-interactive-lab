A GAN in one dimension

Real data: IOP-like readings. The generator turns random noise z into x = a·z + b. The discriminator scores each x with D(x) = σ(w₁x + w₂x² + c). They train in turns: D learns to tell real from fake, G learns to fool D. The generator never sees a real sample — only D’s gradient.

What to notice

On one peak, the generator’s mean reaches the data within a few hundred steps — but its spread often first shrinks almost to a single point (every fake the same value) and then wanders above and below the true SD of 2 as the two players chase each other. Meanwhile the losses sit at the equilibrium values of Math Box 111.1 (D ≈ 1.39, G ≈ 0.69) even while the spread is badly wrong.

Switch to two peaks. This generator can only make one bell curve, so it can never match the data; it typically becomes a wide bell spanning both peaks, putting much of its mass in the empty valley between them — yet the losses again look like equilibrium. Real GANs have enough capacity to represent many modes yet often still cover only a few — mode collapse (Chapter 111) — and the loss curves, as here, do not reveal it. The same lesson holds on one peak: judging a GAN by its losses alone is unsafe; look at what it generates, and measure diversity directly (Chapter 107).
