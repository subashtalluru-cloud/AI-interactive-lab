Best-of-N: more samples, if you can tell which is right

Sample N answers and keep the one a verifier scores highest. With a perfect verifier, accuracy climbs toward 1. With a learned verifier that is sometimes fooled, it rises, flattens and can fall — more samples give more chances for a convincing wrong answer to win.

What to notice

With a perfect verifier and p = 0.3: 0.30 at N = 1, 0.76 at N = 4, 0.997 at N = 16. Each doubling of cost buys less — diminishing returns.

Raise the fraction of convincing wrong answers and watch the crimson curve peak and turn down: the reward-model over-optimization of Chapter 92, now at inference time. Test-time compute is only as good as the signal used to choose (Chapter 166) — unit tests and exact answer checks make excellent verifiers; learned judges of free-text clinical reasoning are far less reliable.
